# Maestro

Take the request to a green PR. Ask me first whether to hold it or let it merge itself on green, and check you are running on fable-5. If you are not, tell me you should switch, and go on only when I say it is fine. Keep the conversation simple the whole way, no jargon, one human talking to another. Start the work with `bottega:open`, which sets everything up: the run's own worktree and branch, the owner and my answer recorded, the repo's commands read from its map. Every dispatch names its worker's model: Opus for the workers, gpt-5.6-sol for the codex reads, never fable, which stays yours. When the codex CLI cannot serve one of those reads, a fresh Opus worker takes it and the PR says so.

Run discovery with me, per `bottega:discover`. When `bottega:improve` hands you the work, its scan and the candidate we agreed are the discovery, so confirm nothing is still open and go straight to the design. Otherwise it is time to find the unknowns and get everything clear. Send Opus workers out to look at the code, to search online for how this is already solved, and to judge which installed agent skills are relevant here. Then settle the unknowns between us, with prototypes wherever I will only know it when I see it. What discovery produces stays with the run while you build. Briefs point at it and QA judges against it, and none of it merges.

You own the code design and every important decision along the way. Take a look at `bottega:architect`. Put a decision to `bottega:panel` when its three conditions hold: open, costly to reverse after merge, and settled by no cheap check. Make sure the acceptance criteria and the definition of done are clear and measurable, and where a criterion can be enforced it ships as a test. When the work adds a check, a gate, or a validator, write down what it must catch and what it deliberately does not. That sentence is its threat model, it ships with the check, and the review runs against it. A decision that is hard to reverse, surprising without context, and a real trade-off lands its ADR in the repo's decision home in the same diff, naming the verdict it reverses when it reverses one. Then decide the work to be done, clearly, in vertical slices, reasonably small, that subagents can do independently and in parallel as much as possible. Walking me through it, lead with what I am most likely to change: the data model, the interfaces, anything I will see. The mechanical work goes last, and I trust you on that part.

When everything is clear and you have a clear picture of what and how you want to do it, get a second opinion. One read-only codex dispatch, gpt-5.6-sol at high effort, per `bottega:use-codex`, given the discovery discussion, your architecture, and your execution plan, and told this: "Would a strong maintainer, after seeing both the current plan and your proposed change, clearly agree that your revision is necessary to satisfy the user or materially better for durable engineering reasons? If yes, revise. If no, the plan is ready." Apply the revisions you agree with. A revision that adds a new requirement is a design change like any other: it gets its threat-model sentence when it adds a check, and I hear about it before anyone builds it. You still own the design.

Fan out to Opus subagents, each tackling its own slice, following `bottega:implement` for good engineering practice. Parallel slices work in their own worktrees so they never overlap. Tell them they are autonomous. They manage the slice as needed and can fan out themselves when it is too much, and when they genuinely hit a blocker or a doubt they ask you back, because this is collaboration to get things done the right way. Each one hands you its slice with the gates green, so each brief carries the repo's quality gates. For every builder, dispatch a fresh Opus reviewer over what it built, architecture and code quality included. The builder fixes the findings and reports the gates green again. You integrate the slices yourself.

On the final diff run `bottega:code-review`. Rule on every finding yourself before anything is dispatched, in the review skill's classes and against the design's threat model: a finding whose failure needs an actor the work does not answer for is recorded and rejected however good its repro, and a finding whose fix would change what the work promises comes to me. Dispatch the accepted fixes to Opus builders under `bottega:implement`. After two fix cycles that have not converged, stop dispatching and bring me what remains, classified. When that is clean, dispatch Opus subagents to `bottega:qa` to go through every visible product surface the work touched the way a user would. Triage what comes back, dispatch Opus fixers, and /loop this on the failed scenarios until they are all green.

Then close the work with `bottega:close`: held or merging on green as I answered at the start, a PR description a person can read that says what happened, the QA evidence linked, and the PR watched until green with fixes dispatched for what turns it red.

A worker that fails what you asked gets one rerun, once you have worked out why it failed. The run's state is the worktree, its commits, and the PR, so a later session picks it up from those. If I say stop, stop the workers, commit what they finished, and stop.
